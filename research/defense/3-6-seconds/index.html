One Targeting Decision Every 3.6 Seconds

The Army's goal for its firing units, and what it asks of the people left at the decision point: lessons from Patriot, the human-factors literature and current U.S. policy
Marion Moranetz
Independent research
2026-11 · every claim tagged by evidence tier · primary documents read at source where available
Abstract

Targeting software has compressed the staff of the kill chain by two orders of magnitude. CSET reports that the 18th Airborne Corps matched the performance of the Iraq War's time-critical targeting cell with roughly 20 people against that cell's 2,000+, and the Army now aims for firing units to make one thousand targeting decisions an hour. I examine what that load asks of the people left at the decision point. Patriot's 2003 fratricides, in which two of 11 ballistic-missile engagements struck friendly aircraft, followed the pattern the Army's own investigator described: rising automation, falling operator training and naive trust. Four findings from the human-factors literature explain the mechanism, and one limits the remedy. Automation bias appears in experts and "cannot be prevented by training or instructions." U.S. policy requires "appropriate levels of human judgment" over the use of force but does not define the decision rate at which that judgment holds. I propose four measurable design limits for the human decision point.

Keywords: kill chain · human-machine teaming · automation bias · vigilance · trust calibration · DoD Directive 3000.09

1 Introduction

"Kill chain" is the military's term for the steps that lead to engaging a target. In Christian Brose's widely read framing, it reduces to understanding the situation, deciding what to do, then acting.5 Sensors and software have sped up the first and last steps enormously. The middle step is still a person, and that person now carries a load that thousands used to share.

1.1Research questions

  • RQ-1How far has software compressed the human staff of the kill chain, and what decision rate is now being sought?
  • RQ-2What happened the last time automation outran operator preparation?
  • RQ-3What does human-factors research say about supervising fast automation, and can training fix it?
  • RQ-4What does current policy require of the human decision, and what would make it measurable?

2 Findings

2.1The staff collapsed and the decisions stayed (RQ-1)

CSET's case study of Maven Smart System describes the manual targeting process as "riddled with inefficiencies and the potential for errors—from collecting the data to processing it, seeking permission, matching munitions, and granting permission." Using the system, the 18th Airborne Corps matched the time-critical targeting cell from Operation Iraqi Freedom, "widely viewed as the most efficient in U.S. military history," with roughly 20 people against more than two thousand. Army leaders now want firing units "to make one thousand high-quality decisions—choosing and dismissing targets—on the battlefield in one hour."1 PRIMARY

The Army's goal counts dismissals as decisions. Dismissing a target is as consequential as choosing one and much harder to audit, because nobody reviews the target that was correctly let go. INFERENCE

2.2Patriot ran this experiment in 2003 (RQ-2)

In Operation Iraqi Freedom, Patriot batteries shot down a British Tornado and a U.S. Navy F/A-18. Two of 11 ballistic-missile engagements were fratricides.2 PRIMARY John Hawley, the Army Research Laboratory engineer who led the human-factors review, wrote that "the fratricides were incidents waiting to happen." The Army "had trusted the system in a naïve manner," had "not adequately prepared their operators and crews for proper oversight of automated operations," and after Desert Storm had reduced both crew experience and individual training.2 PRIMARY

"How do you establish vigilance at the proper time? 23 hours and 59 minutes of boredom followed by one minute of panic."Maj. Gen. Michael Vane, quoted in Hawley (2017)2

Hawley's conclusion runs against the usual sales pitch for automation: "automation does not always lessen operator training requirements." It changes what the operator has to be good at.2 PRIMARY

Finding 1. The last large-scale case of automation outrunning operator preparation produced fratricide at a rate of two in eleven engagements, and the investigator attributed it to trust and training as much as to the machine.

2.3What the research already knows (RQ-3)

Patriot's failures were predictable from literature that was already decades old. Hawley cites Bradshaw, Hoffman, Johnson and Woods, who catalogued the beliefs behind such failures as "the seven deadly myths of 'autonomous systems.'"11 PRIMARY Four findings explain most of the mechanism.

Table 2. Four human-factors findings and what each means for a targeting cell.
FindingSourceImplication at the decision point
Misuse and disuseParasuraman & Riley 19977Operators fail in both directions, over-relying and under-relying, and the two failures need opposite fixes
Trust replaces understandingLee & See 20048Trust guides reliance "when complexity and unanticipated situations make a complete understanding of the automation impractical." The target is reliance matched to actual capability
Monitoring is hard workWarm, Parasuraman & Matthews 200810Watching for rare events carries high workload and raises distress, with automated systems named as the main application
Bias survives trainingParasuraman & Manzey 20109Automation bias produces omissions and commissions, appears in experts and teams, and "cannot be prevented by training or instructions"
Finding 2. Training is necessary, as Patriot showed, and insufficient, as the bias literature shows. The rest of the remedy lies in how a system displays its confidence, paces its recommendations and makes a dismissal as easy as an approval.

2.4The same pressure in 2026

On March 2, 2026, during Operation Epic Fury, three U.S. F-15E Strike Eagles went down over Kuwait in what CENTCOM called an apparent friendly-fire incident attributed to Kuwaiti air defenses. All six crew were recovered, and both CENTCOM and Kuwait opened investigations.3 REPORTED Two days later the Wall Street Journal reported early findings that a Kuwaiti F/A-18 pilot may have fired on the jets after misidentifying them; other outlets pointed to Kuwait's Patriot batteries.12 REPORTED No official findings have been published. By early March, Gulf partners reported intercepting 500 missiles and 1,300 drones.6 PRIMARY Whichever account holds, both describe a person deciding under saturation whether a fast track is friend or threat. INFERENCE

2.5What policy asks for (RQ-4)

DoD Directive 3000.09, updated in January 2023, requires autonomous and semi-autonomous weapon systems to be designed so that commanders and operators can "exercise appropriate levels of human judgment over the use of force."4 PRIMARY The directive sets the requirement. It does not define what judgment a person can sustain, at what decision rate, over what length of shift. INFERENCE

3 Four measurable design limits

Each limit below turns the directive's requirement into something a program office could test. Each is a proposal that has not been validated. INFERENCE

4 Limitations

  1. CSET's figures describe one corps and one software system, and the thousand-decision goal is an aspiration.
  2. Table 1 is my arithmetic and shows bounds; the real distribution of decisions across a cell is unknown.
  3. Patriot's 2003 failures predate current software. Hawley's account is one investigator's, though he led the Army's review.
  4. The Kuwait incident is unexplained, and nothing here is a claim about its cause.
  5. The human-factors findings come largely from laboratory and aviation settings; their size in combat targeting is untested.
  6. The design limits are proposals and have not been validated.

5 Conclusion

Software removed the staff from the kill chain and kept the decisions. The people left behind inherit a decision rate no earlier cell faced, in conditions the human-factors literature has described for decades. Patriot showed that training matters. Parasuraman and Manzey's review finds that training alone stops short. What remains is to measure the human decision point with the same precision engineers already apply to the sensors that feed it.
