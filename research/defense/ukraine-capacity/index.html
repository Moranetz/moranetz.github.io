Ukraine Can Build Three Times What It Can Afford

How Ukraine's defense partnerships divide money, production and ownership, and where the value sits for any venture spanning the United States and Ukraine
Marion Moranetz
Independent research
2026-11 · every claim tagged by evidence tier · Ukrainian and EU primary sources read at source where available
Abstract

Ukraine's own data describes its defense industry as one with more capacity than money. The EU Institute for Security Studies estimates Ukraine's spare defense production capacity at €30.5 billion, of which public procurement uses about 30%. I review the four partnership mechanisms now operating: Denmark's model of funding production inside Ukrainian firms, Ukraine's 50/50 co-funded Brave International grants, Build with Ukraine joint ventures that produce Ukrainian-licensed designs abroad, and an early U.S.–Ukraine statement of intent on drones. In each, the partner supplies money, market access or production space, and the Ukrainian side keeps the design or the validation. Both industries also depend on Chinese components, which Beijing restricted again in October 2026 and Washington has moved to exclude. I map where each kind of value currently sits, and note one gap: no bilateral U.S. program yet appears among Brave International's partners.

Keywords: Ukraine defense industry · co-production · Brave1 · Build with Ukraine · supply chain · China export controls

1 Introduction

Western coverage of Ukraine's defense industry tends to focus on what Ukraine needs from others. This review asks what Ukraine's own institutions say they need, and how existing partnerships divide the work and the ownership. The answer matters for any venture that spans the two countries.

1.1Research questions

  • RQ-1What does Ukraine's defense industry have in surplus, and what does it lack?
  • RQ-2Which partnership mechanisms are operating, and who funds, builds and owns what in each?
  • RQ-3Where does the value in a U.S.–Ukrainian venture sit, and what is still missing on both sides?

1.2Method and evidence tiers

Structured review of government releases, policy analysis and named reporting. Claims carry the same four tiers used across this series: PRIMARY official document or analysis read at source; REPORTED official statement as reported by a named outlet; ESTIMATE analyst estimate or disputed figure; INFERENCE my reasoning. Where sources disagreed, both figures are given.

2 Findings

2.1Capacity exceeds funding (RQ-1)

Ondrej Ditrych and Luigi Scazzieri of the EU Institute for Security Studies estimate Ukraine's spare defense production capacity at €30.5 billion, and report that public procurement, even with EU assistance, "now uses only 30% of this capacity." Domestic production covers around half of Ukraine's defense needs.1 PRIMARY Some estimates put Ukraine's 2026 arms exports at up to €1.8 billion, excluding joint-venture output, and the export licensing mechanism directs 20% of proceeds to an industry development fund.1 ESTIMATE

Finding 1. Ukraine's scarce input is money and market access. Its engineers, factories and battlefield feedback are comparatively abundant. A partner who brings capital or buyers is supplying the constraint.

2.2Four mechanisms (RQ-2)

2.2.1The Danish model

Denmark pays for weapons produced inside Ukrainian companies, at factories already making them. Ukraine's Ministry of Defence reported about €538 million in weapons delivered this way in 2024,2 REPORTED and then-Defence Minister Rustem Umerov said the model would scale to €1.3 billion in 2025, with partners including Sweden, Canada, Norway and Iceland.3 REPORTED The EUISS notes the EU has since adopted the approach.1 PRIMARY

2.2.2Brave International

In June 2026 Ukraine's Cabinet created Brave International as the "official unified framework for cooperation with international partners." Grants are funded through "equal contributions from Ukraine and the partner country." The first programs are UNITE–Brave NATO, Brave Norway, Brave France, Brave Germany and Brave Lithuania, with a combined budget above €100 million. Participation is open to "developers and startups from foreign jurisdictions," and every solution must be validated in Ukraine through the Test in Ukraine platform.4 PRIMARY

2.2.3Build with Ukraine

Joint ventures produce Ukrainian designs outside Ukraine. The clearest case is Quantum Frontline Industries, announced on December 15, 2025 by Germany's Quantum Systems and Ukraine's Frontline Robotics. Frontline contributes "licensed designs," the factory is in Germany at an undisclosed site, and "100% will be delivered to Ukraine in volumes defined by the Defense Ministry."5 REPORTED Licensing implies the Ukrainian firm retains its designs. The terms are not public. INFERENCE

2.2.4United States and Ukraine

In July 2026, RFE/RL reported a signed bilateral statement of intent creating a pathway for Ukrainian land, air and maritime drones to be exported to the United States for testing. It "is not itself a production contract," though it could lead to joint manufacturing of Ukrainian-designed drones on U.S. soil. Separately, Oregon's ReconCraft signed a memorandum with Ukraine's UFORCE to build MAGURA maritime drones in the United States.6 REPORTED

2.3The U.S. gap

None of the five launch programs of Brave International is a bilateral U.S. program.4 PRIMARY A U.S. company can still enter as a foreign developer under the framework, through the NATO program, or through a subsidiary in a partner country. That route is my reading of the eligibility language and should be confirmed with Brave1. INFERENCE Some national Brave1 competitions also require a Ukrainian legal entity, so foreign owners apply through a Ukrainian company.7 REPORTED

2.4The China constraint, on both sides

Both industries still run on Chinese parts. A report by the Ukrainian Council of Defense Industry and the Snake Island Institute found that 38% of the value of drone components Ukraine imported in the first half of 2025 came from China, and motors, lithium batteries and optical fiber remain in high demand.8 REPORTED One analyst told RFE/RL that "neither Ukraine nor Europe can yet replace Chinese suppliers at the necessary scale, speed, and price."8 REPORTED

Beijing is tightening the supply. On October 19, 2026, China's Ministry of Commerce, General Administration of Customs and Central Military Commission issued Announcement No. 49, placing export controls on 13 categories of drone components, including brushless motors, electronic speed controllers, carbon-fiber propellers and autopilots, effective November 18, 2026.9 REPORTED

Washington is closing its own market at the same time. On December 22, 2025, the FCC added all foreign-produced drones and drone critical components to its Covered List, which bars new equipment authorizations needed to import and sell them in the United States.10 REPORTED That rule does not distinguish Ukrainian drones from Chinese ones.

Finding 3. A Ukrainian design reaches U.S. buyers most directly when it is built in the United States from parts outside China's supply chain. That is the arrangement ReconCraft and UFORCE are pursuing for MAGURA. For a joint team, the U.S. side's contribution grows from capital and buyers to include a clean supply chain, which neither country's industry has yet at scale.

4 Limitations

  1. Capacity and export figures are estimates from a single policy brief and from a government at war.
  2. Danish-model figures for 2025 differ between sources, and I found no confirmed 2026 total across partners.
  3. Joint-venture contract terms, including IP, are not public. "Licensed designs" is the only stated term.
  4. The U.S. statement of intent sets up a framework without production terms, and its text was not available.
  5. The FCC listing is under legal challenge, and exemption routes exist that I have not mapped. I have not analyzed U.S. export controls. Any U.S. technology entering a Ukrainian venture needs export counsel before it moves.
  6. Table 2 is my synthesis of the mechanisms reviewed and has not been tested against any venture.

5 Conclusion

Ukraine's partners have converged on one arrangement without naming it. The partner pays, hosts or buys. Ukraine keeps the design and runs the testing. The one contribution neither side yet holds at scale is a supply chain outside China, and the November 18 export controls make it more valuable each month it stays unclaimed.
