The $4 Million Answer to a $35,000 Drone

Cost exchange between attack drones and the systems that stop them, from the Iran war and Ukraine's air defense, and the operator cost that cost tables omit
Marion Moranetz
Independent research
2026-11 · every claim tagged by evidence tier · primary documents read at source where available
Abstract

Cheap attack drones impose costs on defenders far beyond their own price. The Congressional Budget Office estimates that operations against Iran consumed one-half to two-thirds of the U.S. inventory of certain missile-defense interceptors, with rebuilding likely to take at least five years. I compare published unit costs across the exchange: about $4 million for a Patriot or SM-6 interceptor, roughly $20,000–80,000 for a Shahed-136, and $1,000–2,500 for the interceptor drones Ukraine fields against Shaheds at national scale. Ukraine's approach narrows the cost gap, and Russia's jet-powered variants are already reopening it. Every low-cost intercept still depends on a human operator for its final seconds. I argue that cost exchange has a third term, operator-hours per intercept, which no public dataset reports.

Keywords: cost exchange ratio · air defense · interceptor drones · Shahed · magazine depth · Operation Epic Fury

1 Introduction

An attack drone is far cheaper than the missile a defender fires at it, and the defender pays that difference on every engagement. A frontline FPV strike drone costs under $1,000.8 REPORTED A Patriot interceptor costs at least four thousand times as much. This review measures the difference across the Iran war and Ukraine's air defense, and asks what it leaves out.

1.1Research questions

  • RQ-1What does each side pay per engagement, by published estimate?
  • RQ-2What did the cost exchange do to U.S. inventories in the Iran war?
  • RQ-3How has Ukraine changed the exchange, and how is the attacker responding?
  • RQ-4What term is missing from the standard exchange ratio?

2 Findings

2.1The defender's invoice (RQ-1)

CBO puts the unit cost of a Patriot or SM-6 interceptor at roughly $4 million, a THAAD at $12 million and an SM-3 at $28 million.1 REPORTED Published estimates for a Shahed-136 run from about $20,000 to $80,000 by variant and year, and CENTCOM has briefed a $35,000 production cost for LUCAS, the U.S. copy of the design.5 ESTIMATE Cheaper U.S. options still cost more than the drone: an earlier Army order of 600 Coyote Block 2C interceptors for $75 million works out to $125,000 each.7 INFERENCE

A Patriot fired at a Shahed trades roughly a hundred dollars of defense for each dollar of attack. The attacker can lose almost every engagement and still come out ahead, because the defender's inventory runs out first. INFERENCE

2.2What the Iran war consumed (RQ-2)

CSIS estimated the first 100 hours of Operation Epic Fury at about $3.7 billion, including $1.7 billion in air-defense interceptors and $3.1 billion in unbudgeted munitions; by March 4, Iran had reportedly launched 500 ballistic missiles and 2,000 drones.2 PRIMARY The Lead Inspector General's first quarterly report put costs at $33.4 billion through June 30, $22.3 billion of it in munitions, and found that munitions expenditure "has resulted in strategic inventory shortfalls and revealed industrial base bottlenecks for munitions resupply."9 REPORTED CBO later estimated total combat costs through August 1 at about $38 billion and judged that rebuilding interceptor stocks could take at least five years.1 REPORTED

The CSIS authors note that Shaheds fly at about 120 mph without maneuvering, so guns, Coyote and APKWS can stop them: "The drones' value to Iran is in numbers."2 PRIMARY Read that way, a Shahed succeeds when it forces the defender to spend, whether or not it hits anything. INFERENCE

Finding 1. The binding constraint in drone-saturated air defense is inventory, and inventory is set by cost and production rate together. The official record now describes U.S. interceptor stocks as strategically short.

2.3Ukraine changed the denominator (RQ-3)

Ukraine's interceptor drones cost $1,000 to $2,500: Wild Hornets prices its Sting at $2,500, and SkyFall's P1-SUN costs Ukrainian units $1,000. In February 2026, interceptors destroyed more than 70% of the Shaheds shot down over Kyiv.4 REPORTED In May 2026, Ukraine's Defence Ministry counted 8,150 launches of Shahed, Gerbera and Italmas drones and reported 91.73% intercepted.6 REPORTED In March, the Pentagon and at least one Gulf state were reported to be in talks to buy Ukrainian interceptors.4 REPORTED

The attacker is answering. Russia has fielded jet-powered Shahed variants estimated at $100,000 to $150,000 and flying at up to 600 km/h, against which existing interceptor drones have been less effective.10 ESTIMATE In October 2026, Ukraine's General Staff reported intercepting 89% of conventional Shahed-type drones but 66% of jet-powered ones, out of 6,473 launched.11 REPORTED Brave1 is funding a faster interceptor whose launches must cost no more than $20,000 to $25,000.10 REPORTED

2.4The missing term (RQ-4)

Reporting on Ukraine's interceptors notes that a human operator takes manual control for the final seconds of each intercept.4 REPORTED A $1,000 drone works only because someone trained can fly it into a target closing at highway speed, often at night and under jamming. A defender with cheap interceptors and too few qualified operators reaches an empty magazine as surely as one with too few missiles. INFERENCE The U.S. Army describes its own remaining drone work as "getting qualified pilots down to the squad level."3 REPORTED

Finding 2. Cost exchange has a third term, operator-hours per intercept, and each new interceptor generation resets it by requiring operators trained on the new system. No public dataset reports it.

4 Limitations

  1. Every unit price is an estimate. Shahed costs vary about fourfold between sources, and adversary production costs are not public.
  2. The Coyote figure is my division of a contract total and may include costs beyond the missile.
  3. Interceptor kill counts come from manufacturers and governments at war and are not independently verified. The 70% Kyiv figure covers one city in one month.
  4. The Inspector General notes most agency data in its report was not independently verified, and I read its findings through CBS News.
  5. Interceptor-to-threat pairings in Figure 1 are illustrative.

5 Conclusion

The cheap drone wins on the defender's invoice, and the Iran war turned that arithmetic into a strategic shortfall. Ukraine has shown the gap can be narrowed with cheap interceptors flown by people, and Russia has shown it reopens within months. Each turn of that cycle needs a new generation of qualified operators, at a cost nobody yet measures.
